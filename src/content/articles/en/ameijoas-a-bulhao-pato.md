Ameijoas à Bulhão Pato is the easiest dish to describe and the easiest to ruin. It takes clams, olive oil, garlic, coriander, lemon and a little white wine. Nothing else.

No cream. No flour. No thick sauce. The liquor left in the bottom of the plate is what came out of the clams as they opened, carried by the oil — which is why the bread is not optional.

## Where the name comes from

From **Raimundo António de Bulhão Pato**, a nineteenth-century Portuguese poet with a reputation as a gastronome. The dish was dedicated to him and took his name, which is a considerably better way of reaching posterity than most of his verse.

## Coriander, not parsley

This is the line between the dish and an imitation. **Coriander.** Made with parsley it is something else — it may be good, but it is not this, and some places say as much on the menu.

## How it's made

Five minutes on the heat, and nearly all the work is before that.

**Soaking.** The clams sit in salted water for several hours, with changes of water, so they expel their sand. This is the step that decides everything: one badly soaked clam ruins the whole plate and nothing afterwards can correct it.

**Colour the garlic**, sliced, in generous olive oil, without burning it.

**Add the clams**, raise the heat, cover. They open in minutes.

**White wine**, a little, left to reduce.

**Chopped coriander and lemon juice** off the heat, at the end. The coriander is not cooked; it goes in on the residual warmth.

Discard any that did not open.

## What to drink

A cold, dry white — in the Margem Sul, one from the Setúbal peninsula. Beer works equally well. It is a starter and wants a light drink; moscatel is for the end of the meal.

## Where to eat it in the Margem Sul

The rule is the same as for all shellfish in this region: **eat it where it arrives in the morning.**

On the **Cacilhas waterfront**, the marisqueiras by the ferry terminal, with Lisbon across the water. The [Cacilhas guide](/en/places/almada/almada-cova-da-piedade-pragal-e-cacilhas/cacilhas/) says which to choose.

In **Setúbal** and **Sesimbra**, where there is an auction and the fish and shellfish come in the same day.

**How to choose:** ask whether the clams are Portuguese and where they come from. The place that answers without hesitating is usually the right place.

## The honest part

It is an expensive dish for what it looks like, and the price follows the price of clams, which moves a great deal. A small portion going cheap is rarely good news.

And it is worth saying: a considerable share of the clams served in Portugal is imported. That does not make them bad — it makes the question legitimate.
