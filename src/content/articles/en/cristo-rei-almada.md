Cristo Rei stands on the clifftop at Almada, arms open towards Lisbon, and it is the most visible thing in the Margem Sul from the other side of the river. Everyone knows it by sight. Far fewer people know you can walk into the grounds, sit at the viewpoint and look at the whole estuary without paying anything.

This is what's worth knowing before you go: what costs money, what doesn't, and what time of day repays the trip.

## What's free and what isn't

This is the distinction that changes the visit, and almost no page states it plainly.

**Entering the sanctuary is free.** The grounds, the chapel and the ground-level viewpoint have no ticket. You can arrive, cross the esplanade, go into the chapel and stand looking at the bridge and the river for as long as you like, for nothing.

**What you pay for is going up.** The €10 buys the lift to the top of the portico, 75 metres above the grounds, at the feet of the statue. It's a different view: higher, open on both sides, with the Ponte 25 de Abril running underneath and the estuary widening away to the east.

Ages 8 to 12 pay €3. Under-8s are free. Groups booked 72 hours ahead pay €3 a head. Parking in the grounds is €2.

## Hours

April to September, 10am to 7pm. October to March, 10am to 6pm. Last entry is always 20 minutes before closing — arrive at ten to seven in August and you won't be going up.

## Getting there

The good way is over the water. Ferry from Cais do Sodré to Cacilhas, about ten minutes, then the 101 bus from the terminal. You step off the boat with the cliff in front of you and go up.

By car it's quicker and duller: Ponte 25 de Abril, exit at Almada, and there's parking on site.

There's a third route that mostly only residents take — up the Elevador da Boca do Vento at Cacilhas, across Almada Velha on foot, and into the sanctuary from above. Twenty minutes uphill, with the river on your left the whole way.

## What time to go

Late afternoon. The monument faces north-east towards Lisbon, which means that at the end of the day the sun is behind it and the statue goes into silhouette — that's how you get the photograph everyone knows, a dark figure against a lit sky.

If what you want is Lisbon well lit rather than the silhouette, go in the morning instead: the light lands on the city and the river reads pale.

## Why it's there

It wasn't a state commission or a tourism project. It was a promise.

On 20 April 1940, at Fátima, with the war already burning across Europe, the Portuguese bishops vowed to raise a monument to Christ the King if the country were spared the conflict. Portugal stayed neutral and the vow was kept: construction began in December 1949 and the sanctuary was inaugurated on 17 May 1959.

The form came from elsewhere. Cardinal Cerejeira had been in Rio de Janeiro in 1934, had seen the Cristo Redentor above the bay, and wanted the same gesture over the Tagus. The portico is by António Lino; the 28-metre statue is by Francisco Franco de Sousa.

## What's hard to read from a distance

Seen from Lisbon, Cristo Rei looks as though it's set at the water's edge. It isn't. It stands on top of a cliff, well back, with a whole hillside of scrub and houses between it and the river. That's why there's a climb, and why the view repays it: the monument's 110 metres sit on top of the cliff's, and the statue's feet end up around 133 metres above the Tagus.

And contrary to what the photograph suggests, the monument isn't stranded on its own in open country. It's in Almada, with neighbourhoods around it, a car park, a café and buses arriving. It's part of the town — which is, honestly, better than the alternative.
