Cabo Espichel is where the Margem Sul stops. The Setúbal peninsula ends in a vertical cliff over a hundred metres high and, on top of it, in one of the strangest arrangements in the country: an eighteenth-century church with two long wings of houses forming a courtyard open to the ocean, almost all of them empty.

It isn't a ruin and it isn't a restored monument. It's somewhere between the two, wind-scoured, with the sea working away below it and a lighthouse alongside.

## The sanctuary

The complex is a **pilgrimage sanctuary**. Tradition holds that an image of the Virgin appeared on the cliff to two old men, one from Alcabideche on the far bank and one from Caparica — and that the pilgrimage grew from there, with people crossing the Tagus on foot and by boat to reach the cape.

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries it was among the largest pilgrimages in central Portugal. The two wings of houses framing the courtyard were built in the eighteenth century precisely to lodge pilgrims: dozens of identical doors facing one another, with the sea at the end of the corridor.

Today they stand largely empty. That is what gives the place its character — an enormous courtyard, built for crowds, almost always deserted.

On the cliff edge, a few metres from the drop, stands the **Ermida da Memória**, the small chapel marking the site of the apparition.

## The lighthouse

The Cabo Espichel lighthouse entered service in 1790 and is among the oldest on the Portuguese coast. It stands beside the sanctuary, and the combination — pilgrimage church, empty houses, lighthouse, cliff — is what makes the cape hard to compare to anywhere else.

## The dinosaur tracks

The cliffs here carry **Cretaceous dinosaur trackways**, left when this was coastal plain rather than precipice. The best-known are at Pedra da Mua and around Lagosteiros.

They're visible from points on the cliff, tilted to near-vertical by the geological uplift that came afterwards — which is the detail that makes them memorable: the prints were laid down flat and today they climb a wall.

You can't reach them from the beach without risk. Stay at the viewpoints.

## When to go

Late afternoon. The cape faces west and the sun sets into the sea directly in front of the sanctuary.

Take a coat, even in August. The cape makes its own wind and there is nowhere to shelter.

And take water: there is almost nothing open up there, and the nearest services are at Azoia, on the way back to Sesimbra.

## The honest part

Cabo Espichel is in a condition that bothers some people and is exactly what draws others. The pilgrim houses have been shut and decaying for decades; restoration plans have been announced more than once, and the place remains as it is.

If you're expecting a tended monument with a ticket office, interpretation boards and a café, this isn't it. If you're expecting somewhere enormous, half-abandoned, at the end of the land, with the Atlantic working a hundred metres below — that is exactly what it is.
