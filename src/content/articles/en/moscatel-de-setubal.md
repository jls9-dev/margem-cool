There is one thing the Setúbal Peninsula makes better than anywhere else on earth, and it is not fried cuttlefish. It is a sweet amber wine that smells of orange and honey before it gets anywhere near your mouth.

Moscatel de Setúbal has been made here for centuries, on the slopes of the Arrábida and in the sandy soils around Palmela and Azeitão. It is one of the few things this region has with a protected name of its own, and comfortably the easiest to take home.

## What is in the glass

A fortified wine: muscat grapes, fermentation stopped halfway with grape spirit, and the grape's own sugar left behind because the yeasts were interrupted before they could eat it. Nothing is sweetened. The sugar is the fruit.

Then comes the step that makes the rest of it. The grape skins are left to macerate in the already-fortified wine for months. That is where the enormous aroma comes from: orange peel, honey, black tea, dried apricot. You can smell it a metre from the glass.

## Two grapes, one of which nearly disappeared

**Moscatel de Setúbal** is the common grape, Muscat of Alexandria. It gives the exuberant, citrus-driven wine most people recognise.

**Moscatel Roxo** is a different story. Darker skins, tiny production, a finer and more restrained wine. It was close to extinct in the second half of the last century, and was rescued in the 1970s and 80s by Fernando Soares Franco, fifth generation of the José Maria da Fonseca family, who replanted it when nobody could see a reason to. It is now the region's most expensive bottle and wins prizes with some regularity.

For a label to say Moscatel de Setúbal or Moscatel Roxo de Setúbal, at least 85% of the wine must be that grape. To say DO Setúbal alone, 67% is enough.

## Reading the label

**Superior** is not marketing. It means a minimum of five years of ageing and approval by the regional tasting board.

**20 Anos**, or 10, or 40, describes the blend rather than a harvest: an average age, not a vintage. Age statements from 5 to 40 years have been permitted since 2015. A twenty-year-old moscatel is dark, has lost most of its citrus lift, and tastes of walnuts and old wood. A young one tastes of orange blossom. Same wine, different drink.

## Where to taste it

Azeitão is the centre of all this, and the two big houses sit minutes apart.

**José Maria da Fonseca**, in Vila Nogueira de Azeitão, is the oldest, with its cellars and museum in the village itself. **Bacalhôa**, at the quinta of the same name, pairs the wine with an art collection nobody expects to find in a winery. Both offer visits with tastings, and both want a booking. Turning up on a Sunday afternoon and hoping is not a plan.

There are smaller producers scattered between Palmela and Setúbal. If you are here for a day, do one cellar and a long lunch rather than three cellars. Moscatel is tasted in small measures and catches up with you quickly.

## What to drink it with

**Queijo de Azeitão** and **torta de Azeitão**, both made a few kilometres from the same vines. It is the obvious local pairing and it is obvious because it works: a soft, spoonable sheep's cheese against a wine that is sweet and sharp at once.

Serve it **cold**, between 10 and 12 degrees. At room temperature it turns heavy and loses the aroma, which is the entire thing you are paying for.

An open bottle keeps for weeks in the fridge. It is fortified. It is in no hurry.

## If you are taking a bottle home

Buy it at the cellar or from a wine shop in the region, not at the airport. A young moscatel from a good house costs very little and makes a better present than most of what Lisbon sells for twice the price. If you want to impress someone who has drunk everything, take a Roxo.
