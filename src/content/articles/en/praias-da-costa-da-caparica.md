Costa da Caparica isn't a beach. It's a ribbon of sand around fifteen kilometres long that starts at the town and finishes at Fonte da Telha, with the fossil cliff running behind it the whole way.

That changes the question. It isn't "shall we go to Caparica?", it's **where along the chain you stop**. And the answer follows a single rule: north means more people, more restaurants and easier access; south means more space, fewer facilities and more nature.

## The rule of the chain

The beaches have names — Praia Nova, São João, Praia do Rei, Praia da Mata, Praia da Sereia, Praia da Riviera, Praia da Morena, Praia do Castelo, Praia das Bicas, Praia da Cornélia — but they aren't separate beaches. It's the same continuous sand, and the name changes with whichever beach concession is standing there.

What actually changes as you go south:

- **Near the town** — concessions one after another, restaurants, surf schools, and the easiest stretch to reach by public transport. Also the fullest.
- **The middle of the chain** — car parks scattered along the road behind the cliff, each serving its own beach. Fewer people per metre of sand, facilities further apart.
- **Fonte da Telha, at the end** — a small community of wooden houses against the cliff, fish restaurants, and the point where the cliff is highest. It's also where the paragliders are.

## The fossil cliff

The wall running behind the beaches is a protected landscape, tens of metres high, with layers of sediment millions of years old on show.

It isn't just scenery: it's the reason this coast has no building on top of the sand. The cliff separates the beaches from the road, and access is by boardwalks and steps at defined points. That's why you can walk kilometres of sand with a sandstone wall on one side and nothing on the other.

## The little train that no longer runs

The **Transpraia** linked the town to Fonte da Telha from 1960 — an open train running along the sand, with stops serving each beach. Nine kilometres, twenty stops, June to September.

**It has been out of service since 2019.** The line is under sand and vegetation. Restoration plans have been announced, and the current owner has spoken publicly about doing it, but as of this page it does not run.

This is worth saying plainly because it still appears in guides and listicles as though it were operating. If you were counting on it to move down the chain, change the plan: the bus along the back road, or walking on the sand.

## Surf

This is the main surf spot in the Lisbon metropolitan area, with schools along most of the chain. The sea is open Atlantic, with no hills sheltering anything, and the swell is real.

If you're a beginner, a lesson achieves more than an afternoon of taking waves on the head — and the currents, particularly on big days, are not a detail.

## The honest part

Caparica is metropolitan Lisbon's beach, and in August you feel it: the car parks fill in the morning, the back road stops moving and the concessions are full. There's so much sand that it absorbs the crowd well, but the access doesn't.

Against that, it's one of the few places in the region where you can still walk half an hour along the sand and be more or less alone — provided you're willing to walk. September is the best month of the year here: the water at its warmest, the wind calmer, and a fraction of the people.
